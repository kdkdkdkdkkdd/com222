1. Introduction & Characteristics of Health Indicators

An Indicator (also termed Index or Variable) is an indication of a given situation or a reflection of that situation. A Health Indicator is a variable, susceptible to direct measurement, that reflects the state of health of persons in a community. Health measurement is often framed in terms of illness (or lack of health), consequences of ill-health (morbidity, mortality), and economic, occupational, and domestic factors that promote ill health.

WHO Definition: "Variables which measure change." Health indicators help measure the extent to which objectives and targets of a program are attained. A Health Index is a numerical indication of the health of a given population derived from a specified composite formula.

Characteristics of Good Health Indicators:

  • Valid: Measure what they are supposed to measure.
  • Reliable: Same results when measured by different people under similar circumstances.
  • Sensitive: Sensitive to changes in the situation concerned.
  • Specific: Reflect changes only in the situation concerned.
  • Feasible: Ability to obtain data when needed.
  • Relevant: Contribute to understanding the phenomenon of interest.

2. Uses of Indicators of Health

  • Measurement and description of the health of the community.
  • Comparison of the health of different communities.
  • Identification of health needs and prioritizing them.
  • Communicating critical information about population health.
  • Supporting planning (identifying priorities, developing/targeting resources, tracking progress).
  • Concurrent and terminal evaluation of health services.
  • Informing policy and policymakers to promote accountability among governmental and non-governmental agencies.

3. The 12 Types of Health Indicators

I. Mortality Indicators

Includes Crude Death Rate (CDR: ~7.48 deaths/1000 population), Expectation of Life (65.2 yrs general; 64.3 male; 66.1 female), Infant Mortality Rate (IMR: 67/1000 live births), Child Mortality Rate (1-4 yrs: 18/1000), Maternal Mortality Rate (MMR: 212/100,000 live births), and Disease Specific Death Rate (e.g., TB mortality 23/100,000).

II. Morbidity Indicators

Reveals burden of ill-health. Includes Incidence (new cases, e.g., TB incidence 168/100,000), Prevalence (total existing cases at a time, e.g., TB prevalence 249/100,000), Notification rates, OPD attendance, admission/discharge rates, and absence from work/school.

III. Disability Rates

Event-type (bed disability days, work loss) and Person-type (mobility limitations, basic ADLs like eating/dressing). Sullivan's Index: Expectation of life free of disability (Life expectancy minus probable duration of bed disability).

IV. Nutritional Status Indicators

Positive health indicator. Newborn birth weight, length, head circumference. Preschool anthropometry (weight for acute malnutrition, height & mid-arm for chronic). Adults: underweight, obesity, anemia.

V. Health Care Delivery Indicators

Reflect equity of distribution of health resources: Doctor-population ratio, Nurse-population ratio, Population-bed ratio.

VI. Utilization Rates

Proportion of people in need receiving services (e.g., 43% fully immunized infants, ANC care, institutional deliveries, family planning adoption, bed occupancy).

VII. Social & Mental Health

Rates of suicide, homicide, crime, road traffic accidents, juvenile delinquency, alcohol/substance abuse, domestic violence, battered-baby syndrome.

VIII. Environmental Indicators

Proportion of population with access to safe drinking water and sanitation facilities, air/water pollution, radiation, noise pollution, toxic substances.

IX. Socioeconomic Indicators

Per capita income, level of unemployment, literacy rate, per capita caloric availability, total fertility rate, contraceptive use.

X. Health Policy Indicators

Political commitment: Proportion of GNP spent on health services, health-related activities (water, sanitation, housing, nutrition), and primary health care.

XI. Quality of Life Indicators

PQLI: Infant mortality, life expectancy at age 1, and literacy (scale 1-100). HDI: Longevity (life expectancy), Knowledge (adult literacy & schooling), Income (real GDP per capita).

XII. Other Indicator Series

UN Social Indicators (12 categories including population, family formation, learning, income distribution, housing, etc.) & ILO Basic Needs Indicators.

4. Determinants of Health

Health is determined by a complex interaction of multiple factors:

  • Biological (Human Biology): Genetic makeup, chromosomal anomalies, maturation, and aging processes.
  • Environment: Internal environment (homeostasis, temperature around 37°C for optimal enzyme activity) and External environment (Physical: air, water, soil; Biological: plants, animals, microorganisms/zoonotic diseases; Social: culture, traditions, stress, NCD risk factors, labor market, poverty).
  • Behavioral & Sociocultural: Lifestyle, habits, personal hygiene, nutrition, sleep, physical activity vs. harmful habits (smoking, alcohol, poor hygiene).
  • Socioeconomic Conditions: Income, education/illiteracy, employment, housing, political system, social status.
  • Health Services: Accessibility, acceptability, adequacy of doctor-patient and nurse-population ratios, immunization coverage.
  • Aging of the Population: Increased elderly due to medical, sanitary, and pharmacological advancements.
  • Gender: Biological and social vulnerabilities of women (malnutrition, anemia, reproductive health, socioeconomic inequality).

5. Health Economics

Definition: Health economics is the study of the distribution of health care. It is a branch of economics concerned with issues related to efficiency, effectiveness, value, and behavior in the production and consumption of health and health care, dealing fundamentally with scarcity in resource allocation.

Need for Health Economics: Rising costs due to medical advances (organ transplants, gene therapy), increasing life expectancy requiring long-term care for the elderly, changing family structures, and higher public expectations and awareness.

Importance: Formulating health policy, establishing true costs (including patient time and loss of output), evaluating relative costs and benefits, estimating economic variables (user charges, travel distance), and evaluating planning and budgeting systems.

Health Economics Module

Allocation, Efficiency, and Scarcity in Health Care

Definition

Branch of economics concerned with scarcity in resource allocation, efficiency, effectiveness, value, and behavior in the production and consumption of health care. Focuses on maximizing benefits (reducing disease burden) for money invested.

Need for Study

Rising costs driven by medical advances (gene therapy, organ transplants), increasing life expectancy requiring elderly care, changing family norms, and higher public awareness of health rights.

Importance

Formulating health policies, estimating true delivery costs (including patient time), evaluating cost-benefit options, assessing user charges and distance accessibility, and improving budgeting systems.
